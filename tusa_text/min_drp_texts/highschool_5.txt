When the French returned to Indochina after world war ii, they found that in Vietnam the Communists had taken advantage of the country's war-weakened condition to establish themselves in opposition to the French. The fighting that followed lasted almost eight years, with the Soviet Union aiding the viet minh, as the communists were called, and the United States giving some support to the French. The French were defeated, and in 1954 a truce was finally arranged by international agreement at Geneva. Vietnam was divided in half along the 17th parallel. North Vietnam became a communist state with its capital at Hanoi, and South Vietnam became an independent republic whose capital is Saigon. As in the case of Korea, this resulted in the splitting up of an economic unit. The north got most of the minerals and industry, while the south has the richest agricultural areas. The population was split almost in half. The north has about 17.5 million people, while the south has 16 million. Almost since the day of division, North Vietnam has promoted a guerrilla war in South Vietnam. To prevent a communist take-over, the United States supported the weak South Vietnamese government, providing equipment, fighting men, and a powerful air-attack force. Both North and South Vietnam are, in peacetime, rich areas. The delta of the Song Koi, or Red River, in North Vietnam, while not as large as the vast Mekong Delta, is still a fertile agricultural region. North Vietnam also has well-developed coal mines and other resources. But support of communist fighters in Laos and South Vietnam, with men as well as arms, has strained North Vietnam's economy. In addition, a succession of bad harvests after 1960 produced near starvation in some areas. South Vietnam, while not as industrially developed as North Vietnam, normally has a strong economy based on the great rice fields of the Mekong Delta and on rubber plantations.
