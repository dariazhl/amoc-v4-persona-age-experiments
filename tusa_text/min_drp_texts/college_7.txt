Distinguishing characteristics of the English colonies in the New World included the establishment of locally responsible government exercising a relative degree of autonomy while subject to the crown; a limited toleration for a diversity of religions; a market economy; and a mobility within social classes. These factors, rooted in English history, made the English colonies different from the French and Spanish colonies. England's colonial empire depended on its trade. The joint stock companies (organizations securing capital from shareholders under the direction of an experienced managerial group) sponsored the first successful colonies, Virginia, Plymouth, and Massachusetts Bay. Between 1500 and 1600 England's economy grew stronger and underwent vast changes. England by 1600 had a thriving cloth-making industry, a strong merchant class, and a worldwide trade fostered by the stock companies. John Cabot's exploration in North America stimulated England's interest in the New World. However, early colonization attempts by Sir Humphrey Gilbert and Sir Walter Raleigh failed. The colony at Jamestown in Virginia managed to survive. The production of tobacco contributed to its financial success while its political evolution led to representative self-government. The colony was governed at first by a colonial council and later by a governor. In 1619 the settlers were instructed to elect delegates to represent them in adopting proper laws for the colony. This assembly was called the house of burgesses. The Plymouth company was organized for religious and economic purposes. The Pilgrims left England for Holland to escape religious persecution. They subsequently left Holland because they failed to find good jobs, they feared their children might stray from their religion, and they did not succeed in inducing others in England to join them in Holland. When they arrived in America in 1620, the Pilgrims signed the mayflower compact, an agreement to abide by the rules adopted.
