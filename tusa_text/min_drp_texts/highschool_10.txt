In 1957 the Gold Coast became the first of Britain's African colonies to gain independence. In picking a name for the new nation, President Kwame Nkrumah tried to evoke some of the glories of the African past and called the infant nation Ghana. Although the ancient kingdom of Ghana had occupied a different area of West Africa, the stories of its greatness had inspired generations of Africans. Because it was the first of the Sub-Saharan African colonies to be turned over to Black rule, the eyes of the world were on Ghana. At first, great excitement was generated by strides in education, health, new industry, and improvements in transportation and communication. However, after a few years grumbling began to be heard concerning mismanagement of government funds, corruption in high places, and growing dependence on communist aid. At the same time Kwame Nkrumah made himself virtual dictator of the nation. In february, 1966, while on a visit to Red China, Nkrumah was deposed by a national liberation council composed of army officers and police officials. Nkrumah found asylum in nearby Guinea. Some three years later, democracy was restored with the holding of elections. Ghana is a valuable source of tropical products and is also beginning to expand industrially. For food, the people of Ghana grow grain, rice, and cassava. Both palm oil and cotton are produced for export. But the economy of Ghana is based largely on her revenue from cacao, of which she is the world's largest producer. Cacao accounts for over 60 per cent of Ghana's exports. This places her economy in a dangerous position, because the price of cacao on the world market has fallen in recent years. It is increasingly important that Ghana develop other products for export. To expand industrially, Ghana has built a giant dam on the Volta River to provide power for reducing her bauxite ore to aluminum.
