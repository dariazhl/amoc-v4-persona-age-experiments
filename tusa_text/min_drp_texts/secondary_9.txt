Ben-gurion returned home on a Russian ship bound for Jaffa. In the Mediterranean, it was fired on by a German gunboat. When he landed at Port Said, he learned that the Jews were fleeing Palestine. The Turks, about to enter the war on the side of Austro-hungary and Germany, were checking the nationality of every Palestine Jew. The Oriental Jews and some of the Europeans were Turkish subjects, and eligible for enlistment in the Turkish army. But the Russian Jews were regarded as enemies who would either be interned or expelled. Ben-gurion felt no loyalty to the Czars, the age-old oppressors of his people. Back in Jerusalem again, he urged the enlistment of Jews in the Turkish army to help defend Palestine. Nevertheless, Jamal Pasha, the Turkish governor, jailed the leaders of poale Zion, including Ben-gurion, pending expulsion. The prisoners were brought to Jaffa and shipped to Alexandria in Egypt. This time, the British arrested them as enemy aliens. Eventually, they were permitted to leave for America. Ben-gurion had never dreamed of living in America, the golden land of opportunity for so many other Russian Jews. Not too long ago he had worn a Turkish fez in Constantinople. Yet, here he was in New York City with another Kfar Saba pioneer, Ben-zvi. They rented rooms in the Seventies, near Broadway. The year was 1915 and he was just thirty years old. The two men walked the streets of the great metropolis. They observed that many Jews were prosperous, others lived in the East Side slums. But rich or poor, they were citizens of a free land utterly unlike any in Ben-gurion's experience. In this democratic America, an American Jew, Judge Louis Brandeis, was a member of the supreme court. Brandeis was the leader of the American zionists. Across the ocean in another democratic land, Great Britain, the leading zionist was Dr. Chaim Weizmann.
